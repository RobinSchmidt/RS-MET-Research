\subsection{Euler's Product Formula}
Let $s$ be a complex number with a real part strictly greater than one. Then the following sum and product will converge to the same value:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_{p \in \mathbb{P}} \frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{p^s}}
\end{equation}
This is a pretty stunning result and it is known as the Euler product formula. We need the $\Re(s) > 1$ condition for the sum to converge. For the product to converge, we could be a bit more liberal and just require $\Re(s) > 0$ [VERIFY]. What makes this formula so interesting is that on the left hand side, we have a sum over \emph{all} integers whereas on the right hand side, we have a product involving \emph{only} the primes. Thus, this formula establishes a relationship between a very simple and boring sequence of numbers which we fully understand (namely $1,2,3,4,5,6\ldots$) and a very mysterious sequence of numbers (namely the sequence of primes $2,3,5,7,11,13,\ldots$) that we are yet trying to understand. The identity acts as a bridge between the simple and boring sequence of all natural numbers and the complicated and interesting sequence of prime numbers. \subsection{Riemann's Zeta Function}
Where the sum and the product in Euler's product formula converge, they define a function of $s$ which we intend to allow to take on complex values. Riemann called that function $\zeta(s)$ where $\zeta$ is the greek letter zeta and the function is therefore called the (Riemann) Zeta Function. So far, it is defined only for those complex numbers $s$ where any of the two definitions converges. Riemann, however, found a way to extend the domain of the zeta function to the whole complex plane except one single point at $s=1$ where the function has a pole, i.e. is infinite. ...TBC...
